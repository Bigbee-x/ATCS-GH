ATCS-GH — giving Accra's fixed-timer traffic lights a brain.

Deep reinforcement learning for adaptive traffic signal control, built and validated on real Accra junction geometry. 40–98% lower average vehicle wait times in simulation, across 8 demand scenarios and 5 random seeds.

Osborn Dogbe · Founder Accra, Ghana Status: simulation-validated, pre-pilot
01 Problem

Accra's signals can't react. The city pays for it every day.

Ghana loses an estimated GH¢4.5 billion a year to traffic congestion — roughly 71% of it purely time lost in traffic, with congestion-related fatigue costing the average commuter another 30 minutes of effective productivity daily.

Most signalised junctions run fixed timers: a green stays green for a preset number of seconds whether twenty cars are queued or none. The plan cannot see the road. It cannot respond to a market-day surge, a directional rush-hour flip, or a junction backing up two blocks away — and adjacent junctions do not coordinate, so cleared traffic simply queues again at the next light.

Source: “The Cost of Gridlock,” Glima Research policy brief, February 2026.

02 Solution

A learned controller that replaces the timetable, not the hardware.

ATCS-GH is a reinforcement learning agent that watches each approach in real time and decides which phase to run next — every few seconds, continuously. It learns the junction's actual behaviour rather than following a schedule written years ago.

Software-first

It replaces the signal plan, not the poles, lamps, or controller cabinets — the capital-intensive parts stay where they are.

Coordinated by default

Each junction runs its own agent. Across a corridor they learn to align greens, so a cleared queue keeps moving instead of stopping at the next light.

Built for local traffic

Trained on mixed Accra traffic — private cars, shared trotros, and signal-respecting pedestrian crossings — not on imported assumptions.

03 How it works

Sense, decide, act — five seconds at a time.

SENSE

Read the junction

Per-lane queue length, vehicle speed, and accumulated waiting time on every approach — 41 signals at a single junction, 46 per junction on a corridor.

DECIDE

Double-DQN policy

A deep Q-network selects one of five safe phases: hold, or a protected through/left for each axis. Permissive-left phases are excluded — they deadlock the junction box.

ACT

Drive the signal

The phase is applied with proper amber transitions and minimum green times, then the loop repeats on the next decision tick.

No scripted overrides

An earlier hard-coded emergency-vehicle preemption rule was deliberately removed from the system. Every result on the next page is behaviour the agent learned on its own — nothing is propped up by a hand-written rule.

How to read these numbers

The 98% is collapse-avoidance, not typical performance. That scenario's fixed-timer baseline is a gridlocked junction — the AI prevents a breakdown rather than shaving a good plan. The honest headline is the mid-range: 85–92% on the peak-hour scenarios that matter most.

This is versus fixed-time control — the status quo across much of Accra. Against a modern vehicle-actuated system the margin would be smaller.

05 Method

Engineered so the result isn't an artefact of a lucky run.

Honest baseline

Benchmarked against a competent protected-left timer, after an earlier naive baseline was discarded for being too easy to beat.

Worst-case selection

The shipped model is the checkpoint whose weakest scenario is strongest — not its best-case peak.

Deployment conditions

Evaluated with exploration switched off, over full-length scenarios, on demand patterns held out from that episode's training.

Reproducible

Every figure regenerates from a committed script, with raw per-seed rows checked into the repository for audit.

06 What's built

A working platform, not a notebook.

  • Calibrated microsimulation of the Achimota/Neoplan junction and a 3-junction stretch of the N6 Nsawam corridor, in SUMO — the simulator used by real transport agencies.
  • Trained, deployable agents — one single-junction model and three independent corridor agents, in PyTorch.
  • Real-time 3D visualiser in Godot 4 — the live simulation rendered as a full Accra streetscape with vehicles, pedestrians, day/night and weather, for validation and stakeholder demos.
  • Analytics dashboard and evaluation harnesses — live metrics plus one-command multi-seed benchmarking and transfer testing.
  • Runs end-to-end on a laptop. Full 3-agent corridor training completes in about two hours.
07 Scaling

The platform transfers. The model retrains.

We measured this rather than assumed it. Dropping one junction's trained policy onto a different junction with no retraining fails outright — lane layouts differ, so the inputs fall outside anything that model has seen, and in a corridor one misbehaving junction backs up into its neighbours.

So the product is not a checkpoint — it's the pipeline that produces one: network calibration, reward and training design, and the evaluation harness. A new junction is a retraining job measured in hours on commodity hardware, warm-started from existing weights, not a research project.

08 Roadmap

From simulation to a signalised corridor in Accra.

Complete

Simulation validation. Single junction and 3-junction corridor trained and multi-seed benchmarked; visualiser, dashboard, and reproducible evaluation shipped.

In progress

Perception and robustness. Replace idealised sensing with realistic detection — camera-based counting, dropout, and noise — and quantify how performance degrades under it.

Next

Pilot engagement. Present the corridor case to the Accra Metropolitan Assembly and transport authorities; instrument one live junction in shadow mode, recommending phases without controlling them.

Then

Controlled deployment. Hand control of a pilot corridor to the agent under supervision, with measured before/after outcomes, then extend junction by junction.

09 Why Google Africa Applied AI Lab

The gap between simulation and a street is perception and scale.

The controller works. What stands between it and a live Accra junction is cheap, reliable perception — and the ability to train across a network rather than a corridor. Both are areas where the Lab's models and expertise would change our trajectory directly.

  • Vision models for detection. Inductive loops are expensive to install and maintain. Robust vehicle and queue estimation from ordinary roadside cameras is what makes this deployable at African cost structures — and it is the single highest-leverage unlock for the project.
  • Compute for network-scale training. Three coordinated agents train on a laptop. Tens of junctions across a district is a different problem, and access to serious training capacity moves the timeline from years to months.
  • Expertise in reinforcement learning at scale. Multi-agent stability, sim-to-real transfer, and safe deployment are exactly where outside review would sharpen the work — and where we have open questions we would rather answer with Google engineers than alone.
  • A named African deployment case. A measured before/after on a real Accra corridor is a reference point that generalises to Lagos, Nairobi, Kampala — cities with the same fixed-timer infrastructure and the same congestion economics.
